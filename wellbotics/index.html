Wellbotics

A cooperative K–2 game where programming a robot becomes practice for recognizing and managing feelings

Young children can't learn emotional regulation from a worksheet. Wellbotics uses the robot as a mirror: when a program goes wrong, children debug it together, and practice staying calm, trying again and getting back in sync with each other. Four prototypes and more than ten playtests later, we had Ping!, a card game the whole table wins together by staying in rhythm.

Teaching feelings when there's no right answer

Wellbotics wanted children to build early programming skills and social–emotional skills at the same time, in public schools serving predominantly Black and Hispanic communities in Harlem. Five tensions shaped every design decision.

No abstract labels

Five-year-olds can't regulate an emotion by naming it on a chart.

Play and outcomes

Physical, joyful play still has to build sequencing and debugging.

Mixed pacing

Neurodiverse learners and different speeds in the same group.

No "correct" feeling

Emotions can't be graded right or wrong, but learning still needs to be visible.

Support the facilitator

The adult in the room stays in charge, and the system should make their job easier.

The robot as an emotional mirror

The core analogy pairs a computer science standard with an SEL standard. Fixing a robot's program and managing your own behavior use the same moves: notice what went wrong, stay with it, and try a different sequence.

Four prototypes, four questions

My part: I co-designed the core game mechanics, wrote the curriculum framework and lesson plans, aligned outcomes to NYS and CSTA standards, designed the facilitator prompts and reflection moments, and took part in every round of prototyping and playtesting.

The team tried four formats before committing to one: physical play with a robot, robot behavior, AI and a pure card game. Each prototype answered one question and changed the next.

Select a prototype to see what we built, what we learned and what we decided.

Can programming a robot mirror managing yourself?

A garden board game where children program Bee-Bots to collect pollen and keep the hive healthy.

What we built

A garden of green, yellow and red tiles that get harder toward the center. Pollen, obstacle and dance cards bring in feelings: "Frustration Station! Take a deep breath. Can you re-program?" The facilitator plays the Queen Bee and shows hive health through costume and props. There's no winner, only a hive the group keeps healthy together.

What we learned

Movement-based play naturally brings up frustration and excitement during sequencing and debugging. Facilitator prompts were what turned those moments into learning.

What we decided

Reflection became its own phase inside the game. Open questions stayed open: card management for five-year-olds, tracking hive health, and mechanics that avoid feeling punitive.

Can robot behavior represent emotion?

A feasibility study. Could children program how the robot feels, as well as where it goes?

What we built

Explorations that mapped Ozobot's sensing to emotion: speed for intensity, color for emotional state, direction changes for regulation, robot-to-robot interaction for social dynamics. We sketched world concepts from calm courtyards to tile-built islands.

What we learned

Ozobot's own Challenge Mats already looked like our maps and missions. If the form was the same, the difference had to come from emotional framing, facilitation and mechanics.

What we decided

Narrow the goal to emotional recognition and regulation. Keep "build the board together" as a differentiator. Stay robot-agnostic so the game works with whatever hardware a school has.

What can AI do?

A screen-based design probe: could AI recognize and interpret children's answers, including how they feel?

What we explored

AI that recognizes patterns in children's responses, surfaces states like confusion, confidence or frustration, and prompts reflection, positioned as a mirror and a guide for the child.

What we learned

Interpreting a young child's emotional experience needs human judgment. Automation could standardize prompts, but not replace the sense-making a facilitator does in the moment.

What we decided

Facilitator prompts became core design components. AI stays in a supporting role, in line with the team's earlier agreement to weigh the ethics of AI for young children.

Can connection be a system behavior?

A cooperative card game that simulates programming a robot, built and tested before any robot was added.

What we built

Teams build a hidden sequence of number cards to hit a shared target while staying in rhythm with the other teams. Emotion cards change how teammates can help each other. Any team can pause the whole table to re-sync.

What we learned

Early prototypes rewarded individual correctness, which amplified frustration. Making emotions shared and relational supported pacing, repair and solving problems together.

What we decided

Build a working learning system first, then choose the robot that best embodies it through motion, sound, light and timing.

Ping!: how it plays

Teams of two or three "program a robot" by laying number cards that must add up to the target on a shared Task Card, like "Reach 12 in 6 steps." Every team plays at once, and every team has to stay in sync.

  • Sequence cardsValues from −8 to 8, so a sequence can overshoot, undershoot or go negative, just like a buggy program. The leftmost card must be a 1, so teams have to coordinate.
  • Dual emotion cardsEach card has two halves: Happy/Sad, Calm/Worried, Proud/Frustrated, Curious/Confused, Brave/Careful, Hopeful/Disappointed. Players turn theirs to show how they feel.
  • "Too Fast" and "Falling Behind"Declaration cards anyone can play to pause the whole table. Everyone re-syncs by saying "Ping!" three times.

On a turn, a player chooses one action

Program the robotPlace a sequence card face-down. There's no fixed turn order.
Take an emotion actionRotate your feeling, blind-swap a card with a teammate, or trade openly when your feelings match.
DeclareCall "Too Fast" or "Falling Behind" and the whole table re-syncs.

When the sequence is revealed, the robot reacts to the whole team's result

Exact matchThe robot succeeds and the team "syncs."
Over or underThe robot shows a different light, sound or story cue.
Negative totalThe robot "rethinks," and teams repair the sequence together.

There's no single winner. If every team completes at least one task in shared rhythm, the whole table earns a Harmonic Win.

What 10+ playtests changed

Each round of playtesting broke something. These are the three stages Ping! went through, and the change that made the game work.

Stage 1

Sync rituals and robot-like language

We tried
Limiting communication to beeps and buzzes, like a robot, with rituals to keep teams in sync.
What broke
Awkward onboarding, loopholes for pre-planning, an emotion layer that felt bolted on, and math too hard for the age group.
Stage 2

Guess the emotion to unlock a move

We tried
One player sets the robot's emotion from a scenario. Others guess it, and a correct guess unlocks programming.
What broke
Guessing became a bottleneck that stalled play, and the emotion still felt separate from the task.
Stage 3: the breakthrough

Emotions as an intensity spectrum

We changed
Stopped asking for the "correct" emotion. Players judge how much: a lot or a little, and the group compares and adjusts.
What it fixed
Feelings stopped being right or wrong. The group came to understand each other by comparing and adjusting, and nobody had to agree. Every later version kept this rule.

Then, scenarios became the core input

As play shifted from solving a number puzzle to interpreting a situation together, the team tested eight ways to describe a scenario. One consistently affected how players read emotion the most: what the body feels.

  • Who
  • Action
  • Sound
  • Physical sensation and body awareness
  • Color
  • When
  • Object
  • Location

Five design decisions

Each one came out of something a prototype or playtest showed us.

  • Build feelings into the mechanicsWhySeparate SEL discussion disconnected emotions from the moments they happen.What it didChildren practice regulation and persistence in the middle of play.
  • Move from individual achievement to shared connectionWhyRewarding individual correctness amplified frustration and disengagement.What it didGroup pacing, repair and solving problems together.
  • Treat emotions as states of the systemWhyCalling a feeling a child's own trait left little room to work with it.What it didFacilitators can treat frustration as something to work with in the moment.
  • Keep the facilitator at the centerWhyThe AI probe showed the limits of automating a young child's emotional experience.What it didFacilitator prompts became part of the design, keeping human judgment and care.
  • Stay robot-agnosticWhyOptimizing for one device would cut access, and copy what that device already does.What it didThe game can run in schools and informal settings with whatever technology they have.

The curriculum and facilitation

My part: I wrote the curriculum framework and sample lessons, aligned every learning goal to CSTA, NYS Computer Science and NYS SEL standards, and designed the facilitator prompts and card language.

The same game grows with the child. Each grade gets a different level of programming and a different way of talking about feelings.

What I'm taking away

The project's design thesis: connection is not a theme. It is a system behavior.

This project taught me to turn abstract learning goals, like emotional regulation and persistence, into rules children can play. The biggest lessons came from what broke: every time the game asked players to get a feeling "right," it stalled. As Ping! moves toward adding a robot, the team set four rules to protect what playtesting taught us.

  • Don't turn intensity back into correctnessFeelings stay a matter of how much, never right or wrong.
  • Don't force verbal explanationChildren can show a feeling through cards, movement and rhythm.
  • Don't value agreement over coordinationThe group doesn't need to agree, just to stay in sync.
  • Don't add complexity that breaks accessEvery addition has to work for a five-year-old and a busy facilitator.
