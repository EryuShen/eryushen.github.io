How the form of an AI tutor could change the way a group of students builds knowledge together

Every group gets the same 30-minute open problem and the same prompting script, so the form of the tutor is the only thing that changes.

Most research on AI tutors looks at one student and one AI. My teammate Junyi Zhu and I designed a study that asks what happens when a group works with an AI tutor, and whether it matters if that tutor writes, speaks or watches. This is the proposal: the question, the theory behind it, how we'd measure it and what we expected to find.

The question

How do different AI tutoring modalities (voice, text, video) influence students' collaborative knowledge-building processes and learning outcomes in computer-supported collaborative learning environments?

Most AI tutoring studies focus on individual learning, and the few comparisons usually set AI against a teacher or a textbook. Very few compare one form of AI with another. In a group, the form decides how the AI enters the conversation: on everyone's own laptop, through one spokesperson at a shared speaker, or as a camera watching the table.

Two theories, one for each half of the question

Knowledge building theoryScardamalia & Bereiter

Learning happens as a group improves its ideas together, through three kinds of talk: idea generation, critique and synthesis.

In the study: the three moves become the coding scheme for every turn of talk.
Cognitive-affective theory of learning with mediaMoreno & Mayer, 2007

Sound, text and images reach us through separate channels, and only a little can be processed at once. So the form of the input changes what the group has room to think about.

In the study: the reason text, voice and video could lead to different talk.

How we'd measure it

Each session's talk is recorded and transcribed (the text group's chat is logged), split into turns, and coded by two independent coders. Interaction analysis counts who talks to whom, peer to peer or peer to AI. Collaborative process analysis looks at what the talk does: generate ideas, critique them or bring them together. All data is anonymized, with informed consent.

What we expected to see

Our hypotheses, before any data, for each form of tutor.

Text AIHow the group uses it

Everyone asks on their own device: frequent, fragmented exchanges with the AI.

What it may do to the talk

More critique and synthesis, because there's a lot of information to pull together.

Voice AIHow the group uses it

One shared device and a spokesperson: sequential, coordinated, but brief answers.

What it may do to the talk

More idea generation and direct critique, since it feels like a conversation; less synthesis.

Video AIHow the group uses it

High engagement at first, with a risk of cognitive overload and drifting off topic.

What it may do to the talk

More new ideas early on; critique and synthesis less developed.

And how we might be wrong
  • Groups might share one text AI instead of each using their own.
  • Prior knowledge and communication skills might matter more than the form of the tutor.
  • With the same task and script, the three knowledge-building moves might not differ at all.

Reflection

This project helped me see that the form of an AI tool, text, voice or video, can shape how a group talks to it and to each other. It also gave me hands-on experience applying learning theories to research design. Mori, an AI learning companion for six-year-olds, draws on the same cognitive load research.
