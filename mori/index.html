Mori

A child's “first AI” that turns everyday questions into a tree of knowledge

Children ask questions all day, but each answer tends to stand alone and fade. Mori is designed for 6 to 7 year olds: it collects their questions, connects related ones, turns them into short illustrated lessons, and brings them back days later so the learning lasts.

The problem: AI built for adults, used by children

Most AI tools, including widely used chatbots, were designed for adults. Our research started from what that means for a six-year-old.

A closing window

Young children are natural explanation-seekers, but their questioning starts to decline around age 5 or 6 as school redirects attention away from child-initiated inquiry. Ages 6 to 7 are a critical window to keep curiosity alive.

Liquin & Lombrozo, 2020; Salmon & Barrera, 2021
Schemas still forming

At 6 to 7, children are moving from intuitive to logical thinking and building the mental frameworks that organize knowledge. New ideas stick when they connect to what they already know, not when they arrive alone.

Piaget, 1952; Bartlett, 1932
Answers that overload

Adult-style AI answers are dense and multi-part. A child's limited working memory gets used up on the language and volume, leaving little room for learning. Over time, knowledge stays fragmented.

Cognitive Load Theory; Baxter et al., 2025

The idea: every question is a leaf

We built Mori around one metaphor that a child can see and a designer can design for. Each part of the tree stands for a step in how understanding grows.

  • 🍃
    Leaves: curiosityEach question is a leaf, a spark of curiosity.
  • 🌿
    Branches: connectionRelated questions are connected and grouped into themes.
  • ☀️
    Nourishment: understandingCuriosity is enriched through structured learning.
  • 🌱
    Roots: memoryKnowledge becomes lasting memory that supports growth.

The learning design: from question to memory

My part: I co-wrote the research behind Mori, designed the learning experience (what the child does at each stage, in what order, and why), and designed the AI platform that powers it.

The child asks anything, by voice or by typing.

What happens

Every question passes a safety filter first. Safe questions get a friendly, conversational answer with an emotional reaction; unsafe ones are gently redirected. The question is saved to the child's tree.

Learning science

In a study of 103 children ages 5 to 7, those encouraged to ask questions valued new science information more than those who only listened, and children with less background knowledge benefited most. Learning starts from the child's own question.

Park et al., 2025

Design decision

Keep capture tiny. Curiosity is fleeting, so asking takes one short voice or text input, and moderation runs before any AI response because the users are young children.

Related questions find each other.

What happens

Each question is compared with the child's earlier ones and grouped by meaning. Once a theme has three or more questions, it appears as a bubble in a “galaxy” the child can tap.

Learning science

Children don't just collect facts; they organize them into connected structures. Grouping questions into themes, like clouds, puddles and rainbows into the water cycle, supports early schema building.

Piaget, 1952; Bartlett, 1932

Design decision

Themes come from the child, not a fixed taxonomy. A lesson is only generated once a child has asked three or more related questions.

The child's own questions become a short lesson.

What happens

A few illustrated slides, read aloud, move from title to concept to a quick quiz. Each lesson opens by naming what the child asked: “You asked about stars and the moon, great question!”

Learning science

Dialogic reading research shows that stories with embedded questions build comprehension better than passive listening. Visuals that make relationships explicit lower cognitive load for children who can't yet hold them in mind through words alone.

Zevenbergen & Whitehurst, 2003; Cognitive Load Theory

Design decision

Make it personal. Opening with the child's own words shows that their curiosity is what built the lesson.

The child checks how well they really understand.

What happens

The child rates their confidence (😕 🙂 😄), then explains the idea in their own words by voice. The explanation is transcribed and given feedback, and the child earns a badge.

Learning science

Explaining in your own words makes the child reconstruct the idea, not just recognize it, and comparing confidence with performance builds awareness of what they really know.

Design decision

A “calibration gap” score compares the child's self-rating with the quality of their explanation, so we can see whether they know what they know.

The learning comes back, right before it fades.

What happens

Reminders arrive after 1, 3, 7 and 14 days: “3 days ago, you asked…” The child tries to recall the answer. A correct answer doubles the next interval and adds to a streak; a miss resets it to one day and replays the lesson.

Learning science

Active recall strengthens long-term memory more than re-reading. In elementary school children, the benefit held regardless of reading comprehension or processing speed, which makes it an equitable choice for early readers.

Karpicke, Blunt & Smith, 2016

Design decision

Struggle isn't failure. A missed recall leads back to the lesson, not to a penalty.

The AI platform

I designed the AI platform behind the learning journey: the overall framework, which model does what at each stage, and the prompts that shape how Mori talks to a six-year-old.

Teaching the AI to talk to a six-year-old

A general-purpose model answers like it's talking to an adult. I wrote Claude's prompts so every answer fits a 6 to 7 year old's language and working memory, and so nothing unsafe gets through. The comparison at the top of the page shows the difference.

  • Child-sized languageShort sentences, everyday words, and comparisons from a child's world, like the sun being “a lamp.”
  • One idea at a timeAnswers stay small enough for a young child's working memory, so there's room left to actually learn.
  • Warm and encouragingEvery answer treats the question as a good one, to keep the habit of asking alive.
  • Safe by designNo violent or frightening content. Inappropriate questions are filtered out and gently redirected before the model answers.

What the AI does at each stage

StageWhat the AI doesPowered by
CaptureScreens every question for safety, then answers in short, child-friendly language with a warm reactionModeration API, Claude
ClusterTurns each question into an embedding and groups questions by meaning into themesPostgreSQL + pgvector
SynthesizeWrites a short lesson as structured slides plus quiz options, then narrates it aloudClaude (JSON output), OpenAI TTS
ReflectTranscribes the child's spoken explanation, gives feedback, and compares it with their confidence ratingWhisper, Claude
RetrieveScores each recall attempt as correct, partial or missed, which sets the next review dateClaude, spaced-repetition schedule

Limitations and how we'd evaluate it

Mori is a designed prototype. We didn't have direct access to 6 and 7 year olds, so a full evaluation wasn't part of this phase. We designed the evaluation plan alongside the product.

Expert review

Review of the learning design by experts before any deployment.

Process data

Children's recorded explanations and in-app traces: can they explain ideas in their own words, connect them, and make predictions?

Outcomes over time

Monthly comparisons of explanation quality, conceptual accuracy, and how well children articulate connections between ideas.
