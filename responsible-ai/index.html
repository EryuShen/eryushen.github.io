Before It Goes to the Client

A 15-minute course that teaches new analysts to use AI, and to check it, before client work goes out

AI can draft a client email in seconds and summarize twenty reports before lunch. It can also invent a statistic and sound completely sure of itself. This course is for first-year analysts who use AI every day, and it practices the calls that matter under the time pressure they actually work in.

The problem: fast, fluent, and sometimes wrong

I started this project on my own, with one question: what does checking AI actually look like at 4 p.m. on a Friday, with a deck due at six? Analysts already know AI can be wrong. The hard part is deciding, under time pressure, what to trust, what to check, and how much checking is enough.

AI writes what sounds likely, not what's verified. For a new analyst on a deadline, a risky shortcut and a careful check can look almost the same. The course covers the risks that matter in client work, including these four.

Invented facts

Numbers, quotes and sources that look real and aren't.

Missing information

A summary that reads well and still skips the point that matters most.

Biased wording

Stereotypes from training data, like assuming who a "decision-maker" is.

Client data in the wrong tool

Anything pasted into an unapproved tool may leave the firm for good.

The goal

Not to scare analysts away from AI, but to build one habit: check it before it goes out. The course treats over-checking as a cost too, because advice that only says "be careful" doesn't survive a Friday deadline.

The knowledge checks reuse the scenario's tempting moves as wrong answers: trusting the AI's citation, asking the AI to confirm, doing everything by hand. A learner who took a shortcut in the scenario meets it again, out of context.

The scenario: built outside Rise, embedded inside it

Rise's scenario block handles branching dialogue. This scenario needed three things it doesn't do, so I built it in HTML, CSS and JavaScript and embedded it in lesson 2.

  • A clock that every choice spendsThe deck is due at 6:00. Each option shows its time cost, and the clock moves when you choose. Shortcuts save minutes and cost trust; over-checking is safe but burns the afternoon.
  • Consequences before feedbackLearners first see what happens on Monday, in the client meeting or in an email from IT. Only then does Priya, a senior associate, explain the habit behind it.
  • Do the check, don't pick itThe strongest options lead to short tasks: remove the identifying columns from a client file, or find the risk an AI summary left out of five articles.

Three decisions, three kinds of answer

Every decision offers the same three kinds of choice: a risky shortcut, a partial answer that feels responsible but costs too much or checks nothing, and the strongest option.

The job aid: four checks on one page

The course ends by handing learners a checklist to keep next to their screen. It's built to be used in the moment, not reread.

  • Should AI do this? A three-row table matches the task to who should do it.
  • Protect the client and Own it: tick boxes to run through before pasting and before sending.
  • Check for the three errors: one question for each, in the same words the course used.
  • It closes on the course's rule of thumb: if a number surprises you, that's your cue to open the source.

Next steps and how I'd measure it

In production: a 70-second intro video, made in Synthesia, to open lesson 1. As a concept project, the course hasn't been run with real learners yet. This is how I'd evaluate it.

In the scenario

Which option learners choose on their first attempt, and whether replays move them toward the strongest one.

In the knowledge checks

Which wrong answers are chosen most, to see which shortcut is most tempting and where the course needs to push harder.

On the job

A spot check of client deliverables a month later: unsourced numbers, unreviewed summaries, and data shared with unapproved tools.
